Registered Reports in Psychology Across Scholarly Citations and Public Dissemination: A Comparative Metaevaluation of More Than a Decade of Practice

publication
Published

January 30, 2025

Doi

https://dx.doi.org/10.1037/amp0001503

Registered Reports (RRs) represent a novel publishing format designed to enhance transparency and mitigate publication bias by conducting peer review before research outcomes are known. This meta-research identified 119 pairs of original research articles matched by journal, publication time, and research topic using keyword matching to compare their scholarly citations and public dissemination. Unfortunately, despite being viewed as a more rigorous publication model, RRs in psychology did not attract significantly higher attention; in fact, they were found to receive fewer scholarly citations than traditional non-registered reports.

In terms of publication bias, RRs demonstrated a clear advantage by significantly reducing the bias against null results, reporting positive findings at a rate of 62.83% compared to the much higher 85.84% found in traditional reports. RRs were also substantially more likely to share their data, code, materials, and preregistrations openly than their matched non-RR counterparts. However, this improved transparency and methodological quality did not translate into higher impact metrics, as RRs received lower citation counts when controlling for publication days, while their overall public impact on social media remained statistically similar to traditional papers.

One potential concern is that citation counts are extremely skewed, with a small number of highly cited papers potentially driving the overall results. To address this, we conducted a detailed sensitivity analysis by systematically removing different proportions of the most-cited articles and rerunning the analyses. The results showed that the lower citation counts for RRs remained statistically significant unless more than 37% of the regression data or 50% of the F-test data were excluded. Unfortunately, this was not a result we were hoping to see—it suggests that the citation disadvantage of RRs is not only present, but also statistically robust within the current sample.
